20 MW AI Data Center Requirements

What a 20 MW AI training data center physically needs, how its power, cooling and network fit together and fail, and the functional requirements and non-goals that follow. xAI's Colossus 1 and Colossus 2 in Memphis, 15 to 60 times larger, serve as the reference case for what goes wrong at scale.

219 GWh assumes the site runs at full load every hour of the year. Training clusters run close to that, but the load jumps described below mean average draw sits somewhat under peak, so 219 GWh is an upper bound. A PUE of 1.25 is better than the 1.54 industry average and worse than the 1.10–1.15 the largest operators report; it is realistic for a new liquid-cooled site, but evaporative cooling would make it easier to hit at the cost of the water above.

0 · Source video

The video is about a chip factory, not a data center

Read this before citing the video.

"How Elon Musk is Building the World's Most Impossible Chip Factory" (NextGen Manufacturing, 16:52) is about Terafab, the Tesla/SpaceX/xAI chip factory planned for Grimes County, Texas. Construction is scheduled to start on December 1, 2026, so nothing in the video has been built yet. It narrates the build in the present tense over footage that appears to be AI-generated. Its site, schedule and cost match public announcements, but many of its specific numbers (50,000 piles, 47 scrapers, 5 GW) could not be traced to any source. It still matters here because a chip factory and an AI data center share a backbone: huge round-the-clock power from on-site gas turbines, Tesla Megapacks as a buffer, recycled water, and work that a one-second power interruption can ruin.

1 · Physical components and requirements

An AI data center turns electricity into computation, and nearly all of it comes back out as heat

Four things have to arrive at the site in very large and very steady amounts: electricity, a way to get rid of heat, data connections, and the hardware itself. The figures below are what that means at Colossus scale. For comparison, an average US home uses about 1.2 kW on average across the year.

120–140 kWone current GPU rack, about 100 homes in a single cabinet
~300 MWgrid supply to Colossus 1, about 250,000 homes
~1.2 GWpermitted gas plant for Colossus 2, about 1 million homes
Tens of MW in <1 show fast the load jumps when all GPUs pause or resume together
1 every ~3 hjob interruptions on a 16,384-GPU cluster, mostly hardware

Power consistency: the load is huge and it jumps

IssueWhat happensTypical figuresWhat sites do about it
Training load swingsAll GPUs in a training job work in lockstep. When they pause together to exchange results or save progress, the whole site's demand drops, then jumps back. Turbines and the grid feel each jump.Tens of MW in under a second; swings up to 90% of load, repeating every few secondsxAI put Tesla Megapack batteries between the generators and the GPUs to absorb the jumps. Reported counts range from 119 to 208 units. NVIDIA's GB300 racks add capacitors and a mode that keeps GPUs drawing power so load ramps down gently.[8][9][10][17][31]
Staying online through grid faultsIf a site's own protection disconnects it during a brief grid dip, hundreds of MW vanish at once and the grid has to rebalance instantly.~1,500 MW of data centers dropped at once in Virginia, July 10, 2024Regulators now require it: NERC issued a Level 3 alert in May 2026, and FERC ordered mandatory standards for large computing loads in July 2026.[11][12][13]
Voltage steps inside the siteConvert grid voltage down to what the rack uses, with batteries in between to cover dips.13.8–34.5 kV → 480 V → 54 V DC at the rack; 800 V DC planned for 2027Racks above about 600 kW cannot be fed at 54 V, so NVIDIA is moving to 800 V DC distribution.[16]

3 · Functional requirements

What the data center must do, independent of how

Each requirement says what the system must do, not which equipment does it, so that step 3 (mapping requirements to design parameters) stays open. Rows tagged core are the obvious minimum. Rows tagged added came out of the video and research, mostly from the failure paths above. The baseline row comes straight from the given efficiency target, and all targets are sized to the 20 MW baseline.

Customer attributes these trace to

  • CA1 · AI training team: train the largest models with as little lost time as possible.
  • CA2 · Owner: get computing online in months, and grow it later.
  • CA3 · Utility and grid operator: the site must not destabilize the grid.
  • CA4 · Neighbors and regulators: clean air, limited water draw, safety, legal compliance.
  • CA5 · Operations staff: fix and maintain equipment safely without shutting everything down.

Constraints (limits, not functions)

In axiomatic design these bound the acceptable designs rather than being requirements to satisfy one by one.

  • Electrical, fire and battery codes: NFPA 70, 75, 110, 855; IEEE 519 for electrical distortion fed back to the grid
  • NERC/FERC rules for large computing loads (mandatory standards ordered for filing by Dec 31, 2026)
  • Clean Air Act permits for every on-site turbine
  • Vendor limits: rack power, coolant temperature, flow and pressure for the chosen GPU
  • Land, water rights, budget and schedule (Colossus 1 went from empty building to training in 122 days)

Where requirements may be coupled

Flags for step 3, where the Independence Axiom asks that each design parameter affect only its own requirement.

  • FR1.3 and FR1.5: one battery bank smoothing swings wants to sit half-charged; the same bank as backup wants to stay full.
  • FR1 and FR2: the cooling plant runs on site power, so any power design also changes cooling.
  • FR2.1 and FR2.4: warmer coolant saves water but leaves chips less temperature margin.
  • FR1.1 and FR6.2: on-site gas turbines add capacity and add emissions at the same time.

Explicit non-goals

What this project does not deliver

Construction-ready engineering design
No stamped drawings, cable and pipe sizing, structural calculations or equipment schedules.
Real-time grid-control system
States what the site must do toward the grid (FR1.3, FR1.4), not the control software or settings that do it.
Financial investment model
No capital cost, payback, electricity price forecasts or GPU pricing.
Chip, server or rack design
GPUs, racks and in-rack links are bought components, described only by vendor limits.
The Terafab chip factory
The video's subject. Its clean rooms, ultrapure water and fab tools are a different facility.
AI software stack
Training code, job scheduler and checkpoint software are out of scope. FR4 only requires the facility to make fast recovery possible.
Power-plant design
On-site generation is treated as a supplier with stated output, ramp rate and emissions.
Site selection, permitting or legal strategy
Permits appear only as constraints.
Cybersecurity architecture
Covers physical access only; network and control-system security are a separate design.
Serving AI products to users
The site is for training; response-time requirements for live products are not addressed.
Environmental impact statement
Water and emissions appear as limits to meet, not as a full life-cycle or carbon assessment.
An audit of xAI's facilities
Colossus figures come from public reporting and disagree in places; they illustrate scale and are not as-built data.
